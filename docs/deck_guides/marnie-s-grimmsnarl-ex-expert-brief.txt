MARNIE'S GRIMMSNARL EX
PRACTICAL PILOT GUIDE FOR EXPERT REVIEW

Specialist ID: marnie-s-grimmsnarl-ex
Guide version: marnie-grimmsnarl-north-star-v1
Prepared: 2026-07-25
Audience: world-champion and equivalent Pokémon TCG subject-matter experts
Status: pilot; not yet expert-approved


HOW TO READ THIS PILOT

This is primarily a practical guide to piloting Marnie's Grimmsnarl ex. It is
not a claim that one list or one line is universally correct.

The evidence is date-sensitive. The TCGplayer guide was published in October
2025. The PokéBeach matchup article was published in February 2026. The
Limitless archetype page was reviewed in July 2026. Card pools, common lists,
and matchups can move quickly, so every dated recommendation should be checked
against the exact event format and 60-card list.

Labels used in this guide:

SOURCE: directly supported by one of the three sources listed at the end.

INFERENCE: practical reasoning from the sourced structure, but not stated as a
universal rule by the sources. These are the most important lines for experts
to correct.

LIST CHECK: depends on cards actually present in the pilot's list.

SME CHECK: deliberately unresolved question for expert review.

This guide describes card interactions at a strategic level. Always use the
current printed card text and current rulings for exact costs, limits, timing,
damage, and legal targets.


QUICK START: THE DECK IN ONE MINUTE

SOURCE: Marnie's Grimmsnarl ex is the central attacker and acceleration engine.
Spikemuth Gym searches Marnie's Pokémon, making the evolution line unusually
accessible. The common shell pairs Grimmsnarl with Froslass and Munkidori:
Froslass creates damage around Ability-bearing boards, and Darkness-powered
Munkidori converts damage on your side into pressure on the opponent's side.
The October 2025 TCGplayer guide calls Secret Box the glue that holds this
otherwise setup-heavy package together.

Practical default:

1. Establish at least one credible Marnie's Impidimp evolution line.
2. Decide early whether this matchup wants Froslass or a Froslass-free board.
3. Preserve enough bench space for the attackers, Munkidori, pivots, and
   matchup techs the chosen plan actually needs.
4. Use the Grimmsnarl evolution and Punk Up turn to solve more than the current
   attack. Build the next attacker and enable the support board.
5. Treat Shadow Bullet's bench pressure as part of a knockout plan, not free
   decorative damage.
6. Recalculate the prize route before each gust, damage move, evolution, and
   attack.

The deck is strongest when its three systems agree:

- Grimmsnarl applies reliable active pressure.
- Froslass/Munkidori reshape the damage map.
- disruption, gust, or a single-Prize board makes the opponent take an
  inefficient route.

It is weakest when those systems compete for bench slots, Energy, or turns
without producing a coherent prize plan.


STRATEGIC IDENTITY

SOURCE: The October 2025 TCGplayer list is a Grimmsnarl/Froslass/Munkidori
spread deck. Its author describes Grimmsnarl as an efficient Darkness attacker,
Froslass as a source of damage on Ability-bearing Pokémon, and Munkidori as a
way to move damage back toward the opposing board. The article also emphasizes
Spikemuth Gym, Technical Machine: Evolution, Secret Box, and early Arven as the
setup framework.

SOURCE: The February 2026 PokéBeach article describes Grimmsnarl as capable of
passive or aggressive play and highlights its ability to use versatile
single-Prize boards. That is an important warning against treating the deck as
a linear "evolve ex, attack every turn" deck.

INFERENCE: Think of the deck as a prize-map deck with an attacker attached,
not merely an attacker with incidental spread. Grimmsnarl supplies tempo and
Energy. Froslass and Munkidori create thresholds. Single-Prize Pokémon,
disruption, and gust decide whether those thresholds turn into a favorable
exchange.

Your plan should answer four questions:

- What is the first meaningful attack or denial turn?
- Which Pokémon is the opponent expected to Knock Out first?
- Where should incidental and moved damage sit one turn before it matters?
- Which board survives long enough to take the last prizes?

If the answers conflict, reduce the board to the part that advances the
clearest prize route.


LIKELY VARIANTS AND CARD ROLES

The core evolution line

SOURCE: The October 2025 TCGplayer example played a 3-2-2 Marnie's
Impidimp/Morgrem/Grimmsnarl ex line. Recent Limitless results continue to group
the archetype primarily as Grimmsnarl/Froslass. This establishes a common
shape, not a mandatory count.

Marnie's Impidimp is the starting point for the main attacker. Marnie's
Morgrem provides the ordinary evolution route. Marnie's Grimmsnarl ex turns the
evolution turn into both an attacking and an Energy-development turn through
Punk Up.

INFERENCE: One line is the minimum plan. A second line is insurance and often
the bridge to a second attacking turn. A third Basic can improve access but
may be the wrong bench commitment when the matchup wants several Munkidori,
Froslass, or a tech attacker.

Froslass/Munkidori spread

SOURCE: Both the TCGplayer guide and recent Limitless variant labeling support
Froslass/Munkidori as a central, common build. The TCGplayer guide describes
Froslass placing damage around Ability-bearing boards and Munkidori moving
damage from your side to the opponent's side. It specifically says the deck
wants multiple Munkidori in play in many games.

INFERENCE: Froslass is not an automatic bench card. It is a matchup switch.
Before evolving it, identify which opposing Pokémon will accumulate meaningful
damage, which of your own Pokémon will carry damage, and whether Munkidori can
convert that damage before it becomes a liability.

Setup package

SOURCE: The October 2025 example used Spikemuth Gym, Technical Machine:
Evolution, Buddy-Buddy Poffin, Arven, Secret Box, Artazon, Rare Candy, and
Ultra Ball. The author calls Spikemuth arguably the strongest part of the deck,
describes Arven as especially strong early, and says Arven becomes weaker after
Secret Box has been used.

Practical roles:

- Spikemuth Gym: repeated core-line access. Search for the piece that solves
  the next actual evolution, not simply the highest-stage card.
- Technical Machine: Evolution: compresses Stage 1 setup, especially when the
  game allows a slower development turn.
- Buddy-Buddy Poffin and Artazon: Basic Pokémon access, with exact priorities
  determined by whether the plan needs Impidimp, Snorunt, Munkidori, or a tech.
- Arven and Secret Box: assemble the early Item/Tool/Supporter/Stadium package.
  Secret Box has a real discard cost, so the cards paid must be less important
  than the line it creates.
- Rare Candy: an alternate route into Grimmsnarl ex. It changes which
  evolution pieces must be preserved.

Draw and recovery

SOURCE: The October 2025 example included Iono, Lillie's Determination,
Professor's Research, Fezandipiti ex, Night Stretcher, and Super Rod. Its author
notes that the deck lacks a strong on-board draw engine and includes
Fezandipiti ex for that reason. The same article says Fezandipiti's two-Prize
liability matters less in matchups where aggressive decks already Knock Out
Grimmsnarl ex in one attack.

INFERENCE: Draw Pokémon and aggressive hand refresh solve consistency but can
also consume bench space or discard future evolution/recovery pieces. Before
using a discard-heavy line, name the cards needed for:

- the next Grimmsnarl;
- the next Darkness attachment or Munkidori activation;
- recovery after the expected Knock Out; and
- the gust or disruption required for the planned prize turn.

Tech and control slots

SOURCE: The October 2025 list used Yveltal, Counter Catcher, Boss's Orders,
Technical Machine: Devolution, Energy Switch, and Iono. The author presented
Boss's Orders as important against slower decks, Counter Catcher as useful when
the deck falls behind aggressive decks, and Devolution as complementary to
spread damage.

SOURCE: PokéBeach's February 2026 article discusses Budew as an early disruptor
for the then-current fast Dawn Charizard matchup, and Toedscruel as a tech used
in a Regional-winning list to address Gholdengo. The author also calls parts of
that winning list questionable. Treat both as dated tech evidence, not settled
deck canon.

LIST CHECK: Do not plan a trap, free-retreat pivot, bench protection, item
lock, devolution turn, or alternate attacker unless the exact list contains the
needed card and the current card text supports the line.


SETUP AND OPENING PRIORITIES

Before drawing an opening line

INFERENCE: Decide the intended board shape from the matchup evidence already
visible. Use one of three starting templates:

Core pressure:
- two Impidimp lines or one line plus access to the replacement;
- enough Munkidori to convert the expected self-damage;
- no Froslass unless its damage materially improves the matchup.

Spread pressure:
- one or two Impidimp lines;
- Snorunt into Froslass;
- multiple Munkidori over time;
- one flexible slot for pivot, protection, or a closing attacker.

Single-Prize/disruption:
- minimum necessary Grimmsnarl commitment;
- the relevant single-Prize attacker or disruptor;
- support Pokémon that make the opponent's prize route awkward;
- a delayed two-Prize bench until it produces immediate value.

Opening search order

SOURCE: Spikemuth Gym provides direct access to Marnie's Pokémon, while the
Secret Box/Arven/Technical Machine: Evolution package supplies broader setup.

INFERENCE: Search in dependency order:

1. Secure a legal Basic and a credible evolution line.
2. Secure the engine that reaches the next stage.
3. Add Froslass/Munkidori only after choosing the spread plan.
4. Preserve a pivot or recovery route.
5. Add the second attacker before filling the last bench slot.

Do not search for Grimmsnarl ex merely because it is available if the board
still lacks the lower evolution, the hand cannot protect the card, or a
different search target unlocks more of the turn.

Bench discipline from turn zero

Every bench placement spends a scarce resource. Ask:

- Does this Pokémon take prizes, prevent prizes, move damage, draw cards, or
  unlock an evolution?
- On which turn does it create value?
- Can it become an easy final target?
- Does placing it remove space for the second Grimmsnarl or second/third
  Munkidori?
- Does Froslass make this Ability-bearing Pokémon carry dangerous damage?

If the value is only "it might be useful," keep the slot open.


GOING FIRST

The reviewed sources do not give a universal first-versus-second declaration.
The following is inference and must be checked against the exact format.

INFERENCE: Going first favors raw board establishment and a natural evolution
on the next turn. Prioritize:

- at least one Impidimp;
- the search chain for Morgrem or Grimmsnarl ex;
- only those support Basics the matchup has already justified;
- a Darkness attachment that remains valuable if the intended Active changes;
  and
- a hand that can evolve and continue drawing next turn.

Because the first turn may not allow every Trainer or attack-based development
line available when going second, avoid a hand plan that depends on an illegal
sequence. Use the current rules, not this document, to verify what is legal.

Strong first-turn outcome:

- the opponent cannot remove the only meaningful evolution line;
- next turn has access to Grimmsnarl or the missing stage;
- the bench still has room for the chosen support package; and
- the Energy placement contributes to the next two turns.

Common going-first error:

Filling the bench with every available Basic, then discovering that the second
Grimmsnarl, a required Munkidori, or the matchup tech has no slot.


GOING SECOND

INFERENCE from the TCGplayer setup discussion: Going second can make an early
Arven/Secret Box/Technical Machine: Evolution development line more attractive,
because the setup package was built to compress Stage 1 development. It can
also support an early disruption plan in lists that contain the relevant
single-Prize card.

Prioritize:

- an Active that can execute the intended development or disruption line;
- enough Basics before committing the Tool/search sequence;
- an Arven or Secret Box line whose discard and search targets are all named in
  advance;
- evolution targets that survive the opponent's likely second turn; and
- a next-turn hand that reaches Grimmsnarl ex rather than merely producing a
  pretty turn-one board.

Before using Secret Box, say aloud:

"I discard these cards; I fetch these exact categories; this is my next-turn
Supporter; this Pokémon evolves next turn; this is the bench slot I am
preserving."

If any blank remains, reassess the line.

Common going-second error:

Using the full setup package to establish Froslass automatically, even in a
matchup where the source guide recommends a Froslass-free board.


TURN-BY-TURN SEQUENCING

Turn 1: choose the board, not just the cards

1. Identify whether the matchup wants core pressure, spread pressure, or
   single-Prize/disruption.
2. Count necessary bench slots through the next Grimmsnarl turn.
3. Establish the minimum safe Impidimp line.
4. Establish Snorunt only if Froslass has a concrete target map.
5. Establish Munkidori according to when Darkness Energy and movable damage
   will exist.
6. Preserve the next-turn evolution and draw route.

INFERENCE: A slightly smaller board with two live next-turn routes is usually
better than a full board whose only payoff loses to one gust or one Knock Out.

Turn 2: convert setup into a two-turn clock

Before evolving Grimmsnarl:

- identify the intended Active attacker this turn;
- identify the intended attacker next turn;
- count Darkness Energy already in play and in hand;
- decide how many Munkidori must be enabled this turn versus later;
- account for retreat or pivot needs;
- decide whether a second Grimmsnarl line must receive Energy now; and
- confirm that the evolution does not close a better Rare Candy or ordinary
  evolution route.

Then use Punk Up with a distribution plan, not by habit.

After acceleration:

- execute any damage movement whose target is already decided;
- use gust only if the new Active improves the prize map or buys a necessary
  turn;
- reassess Shadow Bullet's bench target after all public damage has changed;
  and
- end with a named response to the expected return Knock Out.

Midgame: one turn ahead is not enough

At every midgame decision, write the next two attackers in order. If the second
name is "whatever I draw," the board is not stable.

Recalculate:

- remaining prizes on both sides;
- which of your Pokémon concede one versus multiple prizes;
- which opposing target can be finished by attack, bench pressure, moved
  damage, Devolution, or gust;
- whether putting the opponent ahead enables your Counter Catcher line;
- whether taking a small Knock Out disables that comeback resource; and
- whether the opponent can win by ignoring Grimmsnarl and taking easier bench
  prizes.

Closeout: stop developing when the win is already available

Do not spend a final turn improving a board that already has a legal winning
route. Sequence only the draw, gust, damage movement, retreat, and attack
needed to make that route resilient to the information currently visible.

INFERENCE: When several closeout routes exist, prefer the one with fewer
independent failure points, not the one that displays the most damage.


PUNK UP AND ENERGY PLANNING

SOURCE: TCGplayer identifies Punk Up as a central reason to play Grimmsnarl and
describes the deck as sharing Darkness Energy efficiently with Munkidori. The
October 2025 list also used Energy Switch as a way to enable additional
Munkidori use when Darkness Energy was not already placed correctly.

Punk Up checklist:

1. What must attack this turn?
2. What must attack after the expected Knock Out?
3. Which Munkidori need Darkness now?
4. Which Energy placement preserves a retreat or pivot?
5. Is any target likely to be removed before spending the Energy?
6. Does the distribution expose an avoidable multi-Prize target?
7. Will the deck still have enough Energy/recovery for the last attacker?

INFERENCE: Allocate in layers.

Layer 1: satisfy the current legal attack or necessary retreat.

Layer 2: enable the damage-movement package that changes this turn's prize
math.

Layer 3: pre-load the next Grimmsnarl or alternate attacker.

Layer 4: place only the remaining Energy whose future value is robust.

Do not distribute Energy evenly merely because multiple targets are legal.
Do not load the entire future onto a Pokémon that the opponent can remove
before it acts. Do not starve Munkidori if moved damage is the reason the
spread plan works.

SME CHECK: Which default split best survives common gust and Knock Out
patterns? Which matchups justify putting nearly all acceleration into one
Grimmsnarl, and which require a wider distribution?


SHADOW BULLET AND ATTACK DECISIONS

SOURCE: Grimmsnarl's signature attack applies meaningful Active damage and
additional Bench pressure. The wider TCGplayer plan uses that bench pressure
with Froslass, Munkidori, and sometimes Technical Machine: Devolution.

Choose the bench target by future threshold, not current HP alone.

Priority questions:

- Does the bench damage complete a Knock Out this turn after Munkidori?
- Does it create a guaranteed next-turn Knock Out even if the Active changes?
- Does it prepare multiple evolved Pokémon for a Devolution turn?
- Does it force the opponent to retreat, evolve, heal, or change the intended
  attacker?
- Does it threaten the final prizes through gust?
- Can the opponent move or exploit the damage better than you can?

INFERENCE: A good bench target has a scheduled payoff. Name the payoff and the
turn. If neither can be named, place damage only according to the most robust
threshold and mark the decision as uncertain.

Do not automatically hit:

- the largest Pokémon;
- the lowest-HP Pokémon;
- an Ability Pokémon already taking Froslass damage;
- a two-Prize target simply because it is worth two prizes; or
- the same target as last turn after the prize map has changed.

Attack versus develop:

SOURCE: PokéBeach describes the archetype as capable of passive and aggressive
lines. Therefore attacking at the first opportunity is not automatically
correct.

Attack when it takes prizes, protects the board through tempo, or creates a
specific forced response. Develop or disrupt when an attack produces no useful
threshold and the extra turn materially improves the next two turns.


FROSLASS, MUNKIDORI, AND SUPPORT-PACKAGE USE

When to use Froslass

SOURCE: The October 2025 TCGplayer guide warns that there are many matchups
where putting Froslass into play is incorrect, especially against opposing
Munkidori or boards that exploit Froslass damage. It specifically names
Dragapult ex and Gardevoir ex without Drifloon as examples in that format.

Use Froslass only after answering:

- Which opposing Ability Pokémon are valuable damage recipients?
- Which of your own Ability Pokémon will receive damage?
- How many Munkidori activations can realistically convert that damage?
- Does the opponent have better damage movement, healing, or prize access?
- Does evolving Froslass remove a safer single-Prize board or bench slot?

When to use multiple Munkidori

SOURCE: The TCGplayer guide says the deck often wants multiple Munkidori. They
turn self-damage into precise opposing pressure and share the Darkness Energy
plan.

INFERENCE: Build the number that the damage supply and Energy plan can use.
Three inactive Munkidori are not a plan; one under-supported Munkidori may fail
to convert enough damage. Count available movable damage, legal destinations,
Darkness requirements, and bench space before adding another.

Froslass-free board

SOURCE: Against the TCGplayer guide's named Froslass-avoid matchups, it suggests
a board centered on two Grimmsnarl ex and three Darkness-powered Munkidori,
played passively early to avoid enabling Counter Catcher.

Treat that as an October 2025 list-specific blueprint, not a universal count.
The core lesson is durable: if Froslass benefits the opponent more than you,
remove it from the plan and use Grimmsnarl plus controlled Munkidori pressure.

Budew, Yveltal, Fezandipiti ex, and other support

SOURCE: PokéBeach presents Budew as a February 2026 answer to fast Dawn
Charizard/Dusknoir setup and as broadly useful early disruption. TCGplayer's
October 2025 list used Yveltal as a low-retreat trapping/pivot-style inclusion,
and Fezandipiti ex for draw.

LIST CHECK: These roles and even the legal card pool may have changed. Verify
the exact card, current format, and matchup before adopting the role.

INFERENCE: Every support Pokémon must repay its bench slot. Prefer it when its
effect changes the opponent's next turn or repairs your own consistency
immediately. Delay it when the liability appears before the value.


BENCH MANAGEMENT

Use a six-role bench budget before placing cards:

- current attacker;
- next attacker;
- Munkidori conversion slot(s);
- Froslass slot(s), if enabled for the matchup;
- pivot/draw/tech slot;
- one flexible or recovery slot.

Not every role receives a slot in every game.

Before benching:

1. Count the opponent's easiest remaining prizes.
2. Identify your required second attacker.
3. Decide whether Froslass is on or off.
4. Reserve the needed number of Munkidori slots.
5. Check whether a draw Pokémon becomes the opponent's cleanest closeout.
6. Check whether the new Pokémon carries an Ability that interacts badly with
   Froslass.

Common failure pattern:

An early bench contains Impidimp, Snorunt, Munkidori, draw Pokémon, and tech.
After one evolution there is no space for the replacement attacker or the
second Munkidori that makes the damage math work.

Correction:

Bench for the chosen plan, not for all possible plans.


PRIZE MAPPING

At the start of each turn, write two routes:

Your route:
- target sequence;
- prizes per target;
- damage source for each Knock Out;
- required gust/disruption;
- earliest winning turn.

Opponent's route:
- easiest targets on your board;
- prizes per target;
- whether they can bypass Grimmsnarl;
- what gust or spread closes the game;
- earliest winning turn.

Then compare routes.

INFERENCE: Use spread and moved damage to reduce the number of full attacks
required, not simply to maximize total damage. A counter on three targets is
valuable only if it changes a later Knock Out, forces a response, or supports
Devolution.

Counter Catcher tension

SOURCE: The October 2025 guide says the deck expects to fall behind against
aggressive decks and plays multiple Counter Catcher, while some passive lines
avoid giving the opponent its own Counter Catcher window.

Before taking a small prize, ask whether moving from behind to tied/ahead turns
off your best gust line. Before passing, ask whether staying behind gives the
opponent too much tempo. The correct choice depends on whether the saved gust
creates more prizes than the available Knock Out.

Single-Prize tension

SOURCE: PokéBeach highlights Grimmsnarl's flexible single-Prize boards.

INFERENCE: A single-Prize line is best when it forces an extra attack without
delaying your own prize route by more than that attack. It is not automatically
good merely because it gives up one prize.


MATCHUP PLANS

All matchup claims below are dated. Use the observed opposing list, not just
the archetype name.

Gholdengo ex

SOURCE, February 2026: PokéBeach calls this a poor matchup and says Budew alone
did not materially solve it. The article discusses Toedscruel in Rajveer
Singh's Regional-winning variant as an attempted answer, while questioning
other elements of that list.

Practical plan:

- decide at deck registration whether the list contains a real Gholdengo plan;
- use early disruption only when it measurably delays the opponent;
- avoid a slow spread board that loses before thresholds matter;
- map whether the tech attacker actually changes the prize race; and
- do not treat a single tech card as proof the matchup is fixed.

SME CHECK: What is the current, list-specific Gholdengo plan, and what evidence
shows the tech improves the matchup rather than consuming consistency?

Dawn Charizard/Dusknoir

SOURCE, February 2026: PokéBeach describes the deck's speed and repeated
Dusknoir pressure as difficult for Grimmsnarl, and cites Budew as a strong
single-card response in that snapshot.

Practical plan:

- protect multiple evolution routes rather than one perfect board;
- value early disruption before the opponent's engine is assembled;
- avoid exposing low-HP support Pokémon without a recovery plan;
- plan for an evolution line to disappear before it attacks; and
- use a smaller board if extra liabilities enable a multi-prize turn.

Dragapult ex

SOURCE, February 2026: PokéBeach describes the broader matchup as favorable.
SOURCE, October 2025: TCGplayer warns that Froslass can be incorrect because
Dragapult lists with Munkidori can exploit the damage and recommends a more
passive Grimmsnarl/Munkidori board in that context.

These claims are compatible only when list details are respected.

Practical plan:

- identify opposing Munkidori before enabling Froslass;
- protect small evolution pieces from spread thresholds;
- use your own Munkidori to undo or redirect damaging math;
- avoid giving a useful Counter Catcher window without payoff; and
- map the two-turn exchange around the opponent's spread, not only Active
  damage.

Gardevoir ex

SOURCE, February 2026: PokéBeach calls the matchup generally favorable.
SOURCE, October 2025: TCGplayer says Froslass can be incorrect against
Gardevoir lists without Drifloon because opposing Munkidori can exploit the
damage and Grimmsnarl may otherwise force two-hit Knock Outs.

Practical plan:

- classify the actual Gardevoir variant;
- do not bench Froslass from archetype habit;
- pressure the opponent's damage movers and setup pieces at useful thresholds;
- preserve the board shape that forces inefficient attacks; and
- track whether your own spread turns their damage movement into an advantage.

Slower Stage 2 decks

SOURCE: PokéBeach describes a natural advantage into several Stage 2 decks.
TCGplayer emphasizes Boss's Orders pressure against slower decks and notes that
Secret Box can fetch a future Supporter while assembling Grimmsnarl.

Practical plan:

- use setup compression to attack their evolution bottleneck;
- gust only when it removes a scarce stage, buys a turn, or advances prizes;
- place Shadow Bullet pressure where Devolution or a later gust can cash it;
  and
- avoid overdeveloping after you have established the tempo lead.

Fast aggressive decks

SOURCE: TCGplayer describes Grimmsnarl as an answer to aggressive decks in its
spread shell and expects Counter Catcher to become live while behind.

Practical plan:

- accept that the first Grimmsnarl may be traded;
- pre-load the second attacker through Punk Up;
- keep the bench free of unnecessary multi-Prize targets;
- use Counter Catcher to turn a losing tempo into a better prize exchange; and
- do not spend two turns building spread if the game ends before it pays off.


RECOVERY LINES

SOURCE: The October 2025 example contains Night Stretcher and Super Rod, which
shows that recovery is a planned part of the shell.

Recovery is not "get back the strongest card." Recover the missing link in the
next legal sequence.

After a Knock Out:

1. Identify the next attacker already closest to ready.
2. Count which evolution stages remain in deck, hand, board, and discard.
3. Decide whether recovery needs a Basic, Stage 1, Stage 2, Energy, or support
   Pokémon.
4. Check whether Spikemuth already solves a Marnie's Pokémon gap.
5. Preserve the recovery card if ordinary search solves the current turn.
6. Do not recover Froslass automatically if the matchup plan has switched
   Froslass off.

INFERENCE: Recovery should restore a two-turn chain whenever possible:
replacement attacker plus the support board that makes its attack meaningful.

When the first Grimmsnarl survives damaged:

- decide whether the opponent benefits from leaving it in play;
- move available damage only when doing so improves a threshold;
- prepare the replacement before spending resources on a marginal heal or
  retreat; and
- consider whether forcing a two-hit Knock Out is more valuable than switching
  attackers.


COMMON MISTAKES

1. Building Froslass in every matchup.

Correction: decide Froslass on/off from opposing damage movement and the
actual threshold map.

2. Treating Punk Up as "power the Active."

Correction: fund the current attack, required Munkidori, retreat plan, and next
attacker in that order.

3. Filling the bench during setup.

Correction: reserve slots for the second attacker and the support count the
chosen plan requires.

4. Searching by card strength instead of dependency.

Correction: fetch the missing stage or Basic that makes the next legal
evolution possible.

5. Moving damage because the Ability is available.

Correction: name the Knock Out, forced response, or prize threshold created by
the movement.

6. Placing Shadow Bullet pressure without a cash-out turn.

Correction: name when and how the bench damage becomes prizes.

7. Using Secret Box without valuing the discard.

Correction: identify every fetched category and the next-turn sequence before
paying the cost.

8. Assuming a favorable archetype label means every variant is favorable.

Correction: check opponent Munkidori, Dusknoir, healing, snipe, and techs.

9. Taking an easy prize that turns off Counter Catcher.

Correction: compare the immediate prize with the later gust route.

10. Recovering the biggest Pokémon rather than the missing link.

Correction: rebuild the exact attacker chain.

11. Benching a draw Pokémon after the hand is already stable.

Correction: compare consistency value with the opponent's easiest final
prizes.

12. Continuing setup after a verified winning line exists.

Correction: minimize failure points and close the game.


PHONE-SIDE DECISION CHECKLISTS

Before setup

- What variant am I playing?
- Is Froslass on or off?
- How many Impidimp lines do I need?
- How many Munkidori can I actually power and use?
- Which bench slot must remain open?

Before search

- What exact future action needs this card?
- Do I already hold the next stage?
- Does Spikemuth solve the gap?
- Am I searching a package I cannot fit on the bench?
- What is the second-best target if prizes changed the list?

Before Secret Box or a major discard

- What four roles am I fetching?
- What is next turn's Supporter?
- Which discarded cards are needed for recovery?
- Does this line still work after one gust or Knock Out?

Before evolving Grimmsnarl

- Current attacker?
- Next attacker?
- Darkness for Munkidori?
- Retreat/pivot Energy?
- Expected return Knock Out?
- Recovery route?

Before using Froslass/Munkidori

- Where is the damage coming from?
- Where is it going?
- Which Knock Out or forced response does it create?
- Can the opponent exploit the same damage better?
- Does the movement preserve my own key Pokémon?

Before Shadow Bullet

- Which Active outcome matters?
- Which Bench target has a scheduled payoff?
- Does the target support Devolution, gust, or closeout?
- Can healing/evolution erase the investment?
- Has the prize route changed since the last attack?

Before taking a prize

- What does the prize enable in hand?
- Does taking it turn off Counter Catcher?
- What is the opponent's easiest reply?
- Which side wins the resulting two-turn exchange?

Before ending the turn

- Name the opponent's strongest reply.
- Name the next attacker.
- Name the recovery card or search route.
- Count open bench slots.
- Count both players' fastest remaining prize routes.

SHORT APPENDIX: TRAINING-HEURISTIC STATUS

This practical guide is broader than the implemented training heuristic.

The current heuristic labels only:

- Marnie's Impidimp in exact initial setup or Buddy-Buddy Poffin prompts, with
  a conservative visible-line cap;
- legal Marnie's Morgrem or Marnie's Grimmsnarl ex evolution options;
- exact Spikemuth Gym search prompts that fill a visible core-line gap; and
- all supplied legal combinations in a supported stage, never only the action
  taken.

It masks the entire stage when the prompt origin is unknown, an option cannot
be resolved, a legal combination is malformed, or the score margin is below
the declared threshold.

It does not currently label Punk Up Energy placement, manual attachment,
Shadow Bullet targets, Froslass/Munkidori damage placement, prize maps,
recovery, disruption, or matchup-specific actions. Those practical sections
are proposals for SME review, not runtime rules.

The heuristic has no gameplay authority. It is a sparse auxiliary training
target only. It cannot change runtime selection, opponents, holdouts, gates, or
checkpoint promotion. Missing labels remain masked.
